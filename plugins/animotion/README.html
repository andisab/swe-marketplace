animotion

Animated scenes for single-file HTML: slide decks, knowledge-base pages, any page you build with Claude. You describe what should happen; Claude builds a scene that shows it, checks it in a browser and looks at its frames before handing it over.

animotion is a Claude Code skill plus a small runtime. A scene is one self-contained element, <am-scene>, that holds its own markup and a short script of steps: things appear, move to other things, multiply, type, count, change state. The runtime plays that script on one clock it can jump to any moment, so the scene plays while someone is looking at it and otherwise rests on a chosen poster frame. That frame is what printing, PDF and PowerPoint exports, slide thumbnails and viewers who prefer reduced motion get.

When to use it, and when not

Ask for a scene when the motion carries the explanation: a process unfolding in order, things multiplying, state changing over time, a request passing through checks, a before and after. A good test: with the motion turned off, would the explanation feel broken?

A scene fits

Processes and systems

Setup steps in order, a request through gates, agents fanning out and reporting back, a rollout over weeks, a form failing on bad input.

Use slideware instead

Reveals and entrances

Bullets appearing one per click, a chart fading in. Slideware's click builds and entrance effects do this without a scene; Claude will say so.

Out of scope

Video and PowerPoint motion

Real footage, 3D, physics simulations, and animation inside .pptx files. PowerPoint gets the poster frame.

The skill only starts when you ask for animation by name: "animated slide", "animate this scene", "add an animated scene", or "animotion".

How it works

What Claude does with your request

  1. Settles the visual language. It looks for one beside the file (animotion/language.md), else derives one from your brand, a drawing style you pick, or a reference you point at. A new language comes with a specimen for you to approve.
  2. Writes the beats: the three to six moments the scene shows, in order.
  3. Builds the final layout in HTML and CSS, then a timeline that hides, moves and changes it.
  4. Checks it in Chrome: nothing leaves its box, the poster isn't blank, every jump in time gives the same frame, text is large enough, nothing flashes.
  5. Reads a contact sheet of its frames (saved beside the file in animotion/frames/) and fixes what looks wrong before handing it over.

How a scene behaves

WhereWhat the scene does
A slideware deck, on its slidePlays from the start each time the slide comes up. With trigger="steps", your clicks move it beat by beat, and going back steps it back.
Another slide, thumbnails, presenter windowShows the poster.
Blackout, a hidden tabFreezes, then carries on.
Any other pagePlays when it scrolls into view.
Print, PDF, PNG, PowerPoint exportShows the poster.
Reduced motionShows the poster only.

A playing scene has a pause button on hover. In a deck, m pauses or plays the current slide's scenes.

Prompting for good results

Claude can improvise a scene from one line, but the best results come from saying what a director would. Cover what you can of these; Claude asks about the rest or picks sensible defaults and says which.

  1. Ask for animation by name: "an animated slide", "an animated scene", "animotion". That's what starts the skill.
  2. Say where it goes: the file, and for a deck which slide ("after the title of ./deck/index.html", "replace the figure on the rollout slide").
  3. Give the beats: three to six short sentences, in order, each saying what changes. "The admin verifies the domain. SSO is set up but not enforced. …" This matters more than anything else.
  4. Name the point, the frame that should tell the story on its own (the poster): "end on all ten agents done, one with a warning".
  5. Say how it should play: when it comes into view (the default), looping, or step by step on your clicks while presenting.
  6. Say how it should look: your brand ("in the AcmeCorp brand"), a drawing style ("blocky pixel characters", "flat line drawings", "a UI mock-up"), or a reference ("derive the style from this site / this screenshot").
  7. Separate real from invented: give the real numbers and names, and say whether Claude may invent example values ("label made-up numbers as examples").
  8. Mention constraints: length ("under 15 seconds"), size, the audience ("projected in a big room"), house rules ("no bounce").
Words do one job and motion another: on-slide text says why or how much; the scene shows where and how. If you find yourself describing the text the scene should contain, put that text on the slide and keep the scene for the motion.

And one that shouldn't become a scene:

Iterating on a scene

Ask for changes the way you'd direct a rehearsal. Claude edits the timeline, rechecks and rereads the frames.

You sayWhat changes
"Hold longer after the third beat."A pause before the next step.
"Make it step on my clicks."The scene moves one beat per click while presenting.
"End on the moment the request is blocked."The poster: the frame every still view shows.
"Only one thing should move at a time."Beats are split so each has one lead.
"Show me the frames."A contact sheet of the scene at each beat.
"Use a check badge, not a red cross, for passed."The kit, so every scene in the deck changes together.

The visual language

Brand colours and fonts aren't enough for drawings: a scene also needs to know what a document looks like, how "done" is drawn, how thick a line is and how things move. animotion keeps those decisions in a short spec, animotion/language.md, and a kit of shared parts, animotion/kit.html, beside your deck or page. Every scene uses the kit, so a correction made once reaches every scene.

From your brand

No questions asked

Colours become states (done, warning, stop), the brand's motion rules become timings. A brand whose motion rules say "no bounce" gets fades and short moves, nothing more.

From a style you pick

Named drawing idioms

Blocky pixel, flat shapes, line drawing, hand-drawn, isometric or UI mock-up. Claude shows two or three as small specimens.

From a reference

Point at an example

A website, screenshot, PDF or screen recording. Claude measures what it can and judges the rest, then shows you a specimen to approve.

Set up an animotion visual language from our brand and show me a specimen.

Derive the animotion style from https://example.com, then show me a specimen. Don't copy their mascot.

A reference teaches construction rules, never content: "blocky flat figure, expression in the eyes only" is fine to learn; another company's logo or character is not, and Claude lists those under "do not reproduce".

Outside sources

The plugin keeps an index of about 145 online sources: icon sets (Lucide, Tabler, Phosphor, pixel sets), illustration and character libraries, palettes, motion systems, animation catalogues, exemplars to study and style-reference sites. It holds links and licence terms, never copies. When a scene needs an icon or a character, Claude fetches only that item from a source whose licence allows it, restyles it to the language, embeds it in the page (which stays offline) and adds the credit. Some popular libraries forbid automated fetching (unDraw, ManyPixels); Claude won't fetch from them, but you can download a file yourself and hand it over.

Licences change, so the index is re-checked by hand on request; index/MAINTAINING.md has the procedure.

Commands

Claude runs these for you; they're here for when you want to check something yourself. $AM is the plugin's folder.

node $AM/scripts/cli.js inline page.html                       # add or refresh the runtime and the kit
node $AM/scripts/cli.js check page.html --render               # checks, in Chrome
node $AM/scripts/cli.js frames page.html --scene name          # a contact sheet in animotion/frames/
node $AM/scripts/cli.js sources --idiom line --use fetch-embed # what the index offers for a drawing style
node $AM/scripts/cli.js list page.html                         # the scenes, their length and labels

Needs Node 22 or newer; check --render, frames and list also need Chrome, Edge, Brave or Chromium. Slideware decks need the knowledgeware plugin too.

Troubleshooting

You seeWhy, and what to do
The scene shows its final layout and never movesThe runtime isn't in the page, or the timeline failed. Ask Claude to run check; it names the problem.
It doesn't animate when I open the deckReduced motion is on in your system settings, or the scene is on another slide. Scenes driven by clicks only step while presenting (press P).
Something lands in the wrong placeMoves go to anchor elements in the final layout. Ask Claude to keep the layout final and let the timeline hide things.
The PDF shows the wrong momentThat's the poster. Ask for a different one ("end on the moment …").
I can't edit a scene in slideware's editorYou can move and resize it as one element; change its contents through Claude.
